# 9. Strategy: what the banks actually ask

Every number in this chapter was counted from the banks in this platform, not
taken from exam folklore. Where a familiar rule turned out not to work, it says
so, because a rule that does not discriminate is worse than no rule at all: it
spends attention and hands back false confidence.

Three figures to start with, because they frame everything else:

- **74 of the 120 real numerical questions need more than one step.** Only 46
  are a single calculation. Whatever you practise, practise chains.
- **64 % of wrong verbal options are not false. They are unsupported.** They
  say something the passage never commits to. The question to ask of an option
  is not "is this true?" but "is this *in there*?"
- **Shading, not rotation, is the most frequent attribute in abstract
  reasoning**: 64 % of the 120 series against rotation's 40 %. Most people look
  for rotation first and lose seconds doing it.

## The time budget

From the official competition notices, not estimated:

| Test | Questions | Minutes | Per question | Pass mark |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Verbal reasoning | 20 | 35 | **105 s** | 10/20 |
| Numerical reasoning | 10 | 20 | **120 s** | — |
| Abstract reasoning | 10 | 10 | **60 s** | — |
| Field-related MCQ | 30 | 40 | **80 s** | 15/30 *and* a ranking place |
| EUFTE written test | 1 | 40 | — | 5/10 |

**Numerical and abstract are marked together: 10 out of 20 combined.** That is
the single most useful piece of structure in the whole procedure, and it cuts
both ways. A weak abstract score can be carried by numerical and the reverse,
so the two should be prepared as one subject with one budget — but abstract
gives you 60 seconds per question against numerical's 120, so a minute spent
raising abstract accuracy buys twice what the same minute buys in numerical.
If you are short of preparation time, abstract is the better investment.

**The field MCQ is the only test where passing is not enough.** 15/30 is the
floor; you also have to rank among the best in your field. Treat the floor as
irrelevant and aim at the ranking.

## Numerical reasoning: twelve families

Counted over the 120 questions of the real ORSEU bank, classified by the method
the book itself names in each worked answer. A question can belong to more than
one family — 64 of the 120 do, which is the point.

| Family | Questions | How to recognise it |
|---|---|---|
| Percentage of a quantity, share of a total | 62 | "what percentage of", a column of percentages that may not add to 100 |
| Proportionality / rule of three | 23 | "per 10,000 inhabitants", "per capita", density, any rate per unit |
| Absolute vs relative difference | 22 | **"that is, N more than"** — a stated difference you have to work backwards from |
| Undoing a percentage | 20 | price before tax, value before the increase; you *divide* by 1+r, never subtract r |
| Averages, simple and weighted | 15 | "on average", "per month over the quarter" |
| Relative change | 13 | "by how much did it rise", the answer is a percentage |
| Hours, time zones, elapsed time | 11 | local times, departure and arrival, quarters vs months |
| Unit conversion, or units that disagree | 8 | km/h against knots against m/s; tonnes against kilos |
| Rate and work | 7 | "takes 20 minutes to answer 10 questions", labour minutes per item |
| Compound growth | 3 | "rises 0.75 % **each** day" — a power, not a multiplication |
| Fractions, and fractions of fractions | 3 | "two thirds of the accidents, and two fifths of those" |
| Missing figure you have to derive | 4 | an obscured cell, percentages that do not total 100 |

Four of these deserve their own warning.

**The rule of three is the workhorse, and its trap is the unit.** 23 questions
turn on "per 10,000 inhabitants" or similar. Write the proportion out with its
units attached — 525 prisoners / 10,000 inhabitants = 452,760 / P — and the
cross-multiplication cannot go the wrong way round. Done in your head, it goes
the wrong way round about a third of the time.

**Undoing a percentage is not subtracting it.** A litre at €1.36 including 85 %
tax costs 1.36 / 1.85 before tax, not 1.36 × 0.15. Twenty questions do this, and
the distractors are built from the subtraction. If an option looks suspiciously
round, it is probably the subtraction.

**"That is, N more than" is an instruction, not decoration.** In 22 questions
the stem hands you a difference and expects you to reconstruct the earlier
figure. "In 2010 there were 475,398 prisoners, that is 5 % more than in 2005"
means 475,398 / 1.05, and the whole question hangs off getting that division the
right way round.

**Compound growth is a power.** "Rises 0.75 % each day, five days" is
× 1.0075⁵, not × 1.0375. Only 3 questions of 120, but they are free marks if you
recognise them and guaranteed losses if you do not.

### The arithmetic order that saves the most time

The book's own worked answers declare how many phases each question needs:

| Phases | Questions |
|---|---|
| One | 46 |
| Two | 36 |
| Three | 26 |
| Four | 3 |
| Unstated | 9 |

So **two questions in three are chains**, and a chain fails at its joints. Before
calculating anything, write down the phases as a list — "1. prisoners in 2005.
2. population from the rate." Thirty seconds of planning on a 120-second budget
is not a luxury; it is what stops you computing the right number for the wrong
quantity.

### "Impossible to know" is almost never the answer

The fifth option is some form of "impossible to know" throughout the bank.
**In 120 questions it is correct once.** Question 99.

That is the most exploitable fact in this chapter. The option exists to catch
panic: when a question looks unanswerable at 90 seconds, it is inviting you.
Read it as a near-certain distractor and go back to the table — the figure you
think is missing is usually derivable from a row you have not used yet.

### The six traps the book flags itself

The source marks its own tripwires with "Attention". All six are the same
mistake wearing different clothes: **a column does not mean what its heading
suggests.**

- **Q4** — homicides, attempted homicides and assaults do *not* add up to all
  offences. The remainder is not zero.
- **Q82** — the citrus percentages do not total 100 %, so you cannot get total
  production by adding oranges, clementines and lemons.
- **Q43** — the table is a *running total* of days lost since 1 January, not
  days lost per month. 2,400 is January-to-March, not March.
- **Q10** — people born before 1970 are not the same set as people aged 40 and
  over; the two groups overlap but neither contains the other.
- **Q56** — extraction cost is per kilo and refining cost per tonne. Convert
  first or the sum is meaningless.
- **Q57** — a rule in the preamble gates the whole calculation.

The common defence is one habit: **before using a column, say out loud what one
cell of it means, including its unit and its period.** Four of these six traps
die on contact with that sentence.

### The five shortcuts the book flags itself

Marked "Tip" in the source, and they share a shape: *the question asks which,
not how much.*

- 8 questions ask only which country, year or category is highest or lowest. You
  do not need the values, only the order — and often not even all of them.
- **Only one option can be correct.** If two candidates tie on your calculation,
  both are wrong and you can skip them (Q60 turns on exactly this).
- A proportion visible in a chart can replace a calculation: if one band is
  15 % and another 45 %, the second is three times the first and you are done
  (Q23).
- For "during which period did it grow fastest", read the *slope* of the curve.
  Reading values and subtracting them costs four times as long and answers the
  same question (Q63).

## Verbal reasoning: is it in the text, or is it merely true?

Measured over the 180-question bank, where the source gives a verdict for every
option: 720 options, 540 of them wrong.

| Why a wrong option is wrong | Count | Share |
|---|---|---|
| **Cannot be known** — the passage never says it | 345 | **63.9 %** |
| False — the passage says the opposite | 195 | 36.1 % |

Nearly two thirds of distractors are not lies. They are statements that are
plausible, often true in the world, and simply absent from the passage. This is
why subject knowledge hurts here: the better you know the topic, the more
confidently you will endorse a sentence the text never wrote.

**The operative question is "which line of the passage says this?"** If you
cannot point at one, the option is wrong, however reasonable it sounds.

### Three familiar rules, tested

Folklore says three things about options like these. Two of them survive
contact with the data, barely, and one does not.

**"The option with *only*, *always* or *never* is wrong."** Partly true.
Absolute words appear in 15.4 % of distractors and 7.8 % of correct options
(z = 2.6, so the difference is real). If you discard an option because it
contains one, you are right 85.6 % of the time — against 75 % for discarding a
random option. **A ten-point edge: good as a tiebreaker, dangerous as a rule.**
It would wrongly discard the correct answer in 14 of 180 questions.

**"The correct option is the longest."** No. The longest option is correct
32.2 % of the time against 25 % by chance. Not worth a second of your attention.

**"The correct option is the one closest to the passage's own words."** Yes,
and this is the strongest of the three. Measuring the share of an option's
vocabulary that also appears in the passage, the top-overlap option is correct
**40.6 %** of the time against 25 % by chance (z = 4.8), and the
lowest-overlap option only 19.4 %. It follows directly from the table above:
the correct answer restates the passage, the distractors import new material.

Combine the two that work and you get the practical method: **the answer tends
to be the dull one that adds nothing.** An option that teaches you something new
about the subject is usually the trap.

### Pace

Passages run 121 to 219 words, median 177. At 105 seconds per question that is
about 40 seconds of reading and a minute to decide. Read the passage once
properly rather than twice quickly; four options against a half-read passage is
where the "cannot be known" distractors collect their victims.

## Abstract reasoning: name the attributes before you look at the options

Counted over the 120 series of the real bank, by what each worked answer says is
cycling.

| Attribute | Series | Share |
|---|---|---|
| **Fill or shading** (black / grey / white / hatched) | 77 | **64.2 %** |
| Position within the frame | 51 | 42.5 % |
| Rotation of a shape or arrow | 48 | 40.0 % |
| Alternation between two states | 35 | 29.2 % |
| Size or nesting (inside/outside, large/small) | 30 | 25.0 % |
| How many figures there are | 21 | 17.5 % |
| A window sliding over a longer sequence | 13 | 10.8 % |
| Shape cycle (triangle → circle → square) | 7 | 5.8 % |
| One figure hiding another | 4 | 3.3 % |
| Symmetry or reflection | 4 | 3.3 % |

**Look at the fill first.** It is the most frequent attribute by a wide margin
and the cheapest to read: you do not have to measure anything, only name a
colour. Rotation is third, not first, and it is the one that eats time.

**Expect more than one attribute.** The average series carries 2.4 independent
ones, and 91 of 120 carry two or more:

| Independent attributes | Series |
|---|---|
| One | 23 |
| Two | 40 |
| Three | 29 |
| Four | 13 |
| Five | 6 |
| Six | 3 |

They advance *independently*: the shape may cycle with period 3 while the fill
moves in blocks of two and an arrow turns 45° each panel. Trying to see the
whole thing as one rule is what makes these feel impossible. Reading them one
attribute at a time, in 60 seconds, is routine.

When rotation is involved it is almost always **45° or 90°** — 27 and 17 series
respectively, against a handful at 60°, 120° and 180°.

### The method that fits in 60 seconds

1. **Name the attributes out loud** before looking at the options: shape, fill,
   position, extras. Ten seconds.
2. **Track one attribute across all five panels** and predict the sixth. Not all
   attributes at once.
3. **Use that single prediction to cut the options.** Usually it leaves two.
4. **Take the second attribute only to break the tie.** Series 8 of the bank is
   the model case: shape and fill leave C and E equally possible, and only the
   phase of the wave at the top separates them.

Step 3 is where the time is won. Predicting the whole panel before looking is
satisfying and slow; cutting five options to two with the cheapest attribute is
what fits the budget.

## Field-related MCQ: the ranking test

Eight topics per field, and the bank's own topic headings are the syllabus. For
cybersecurity:

1. Risk management, assurance and maturity
2. Vulnerability management and security testing
3. Security architecture and controls
4. Cryptography, secure development and DevSecOps
5. Monitoring, detection and threat intelligence
6. Incident response and digital forensics
7. EU cybersecurity law, policy and governance
8. Identity, access management and trust services

Plus, in the extended bank: EU institutional cybersecurity (CERT-EU, the IICB,
Regulation 2023/2841), security strategy and portfolio, and awareness and
advisory work.

The questions are mostly of two shapes: **a definition to discriminate** (four
complete definitions, one internally correct) and **a scenario** (a situation,
then what it means or what you do first). The distractors, by what the
explanations say about them, mostly **swap two terms** for each other, **invert
the order of steps**, or **overstate** a true statement into an absolute.

So the field-specific version of the verbal rule: for a definition question, do
not ask which option sounds most expert. Ask **which option would still be
correct if you swapped two of its nouns** — that is the one the generator built
by swapping.

### A warning about this bank, not about the exam

**In our five field banks the longest option is the correct one 95–100 % of the
time.** 437 of 442 questions. Measured, not estimated: cybersecurity 120/120,
ICT infrastructure 80/80, clouds and networks 80/80, data science 81/82, ICT
project management 76/80.

That is a defect in the bank, not a tactic for the exam. These banks were
generated, and the generator wrote careful complete correct answers and short
lazy distractors. Two consequences, both bad:

- **You can score 100 % without reading the questions**, which makes your
  practice score meaningless as a signal of readiness — and that signal is what
  you use to decide whether you are prepared.
- **It trains the wrong reflex.** The real EPSO field MCQ has no such tell, and
  the habit of answering on shape instead of content will cost you in the one
  test that is scored on ranking.

Until the distractors are rewritten, practise these banks with the option
lengths hidden from yourself: read the stem, decide what the right answer should
say, *then* look. If your answer matches the longest option, fine — but you want
to arrive there by content.

The reasoning banks have no such problem, for what it is worth: the longest
option is correct 32 % of the time in verbal, 24 % in abstract and 14.5 % in
numerical. They come from real books and official sample questions.

## What does not work, measured

**There is no usable letter bias anywhere.** Tested with a chi-squared test on
every bank:

| Bank | Distribution | χ² | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|
| Numerical, real | A 14 · B 31 · C 29 · D 25 · E 21 | 7.67 (crit. 9.49) | no bias |
| Verbal, 180 | A 32 · B 51 · C 48 · D 49 | 5.11 (crit. 7.81) | no bias |
| Abstract, real | A 25 · B 21 · C 25 · D 23 · E 26 | 0.67 (crit. 9.49) | no bias |
| Field MCQ, 442 | A 111 · B 111 · C 110 · D 110 | 0.01 (crit. 7.81) | no bias |

The numerical bank's a) looks thin at 14 against an expected 24, and on its own
that cell is at z = −2.3. But it is one cell picked out of five after seeing the
data, and the distribution as a whole passes. **Do not play letter games.** If
you must guess, guess and move on; the letter carries no information.

## The short version

- Plan the phases before calculating. Two numerical questions in three are
  chains, and chains fail at the joints.
- Divide, do not subtract, to undo a percentage.
- "Impossible to know" is right once in 120. It is there for your panic.
- Say what one cell of a column means, with its unit and its period, before
  using it.
- In verbal, point at the line. An option you cannot point at is wrong even when
  it is true.
- The dull option that adds nothing is usually the answer.
- In abstract, read the fill first, predict one attribute, cut the options, and
  only then take the second attribute.
- Abstract is worth twice numerical per minute of study, and they share one
  10/20.
- Ignore the letters.
